In NSW, homeschooling is legal and growing — but requires mandatory NESA registration for children aged 6–17. Adaptogens for Stress: 25-Study Systematic Review Settles Which Herbs Have Real Evidence
A ScienceDirect systematic review and meta-analysis spanning 25 studies examined nine adaptogenic herbs — Bacopa monnieri, Eleutherococcus senticosus, Eurycoma longifolia, Gynostemma pentaphyllum, Lepidium peruvianum, Ocimum sanctum (tulsi), Panax ginseng, Rhodiola rosea, and Withania somnifera (ashwagandha) — for stress-reduction outcomes. Rhodiola rosea and ashwagandha showed the most consistent cortisol-lowering effects. Bacopa had strong memory and cognition support. Tulsi showed the weakest evidence in this specific context despite its widespread use.
Why it matters: Evidence quality varies enormously across adaptogens — many popular garden herbs have strong traditional use but thin clinical trial data. This review is the most useful single reference for deciding what to prioritise in an apothecary garden based on actual evidence rather than wellness-influencer consensus.
🎯 Why this matters to you You’re planning your apothecary garden now — Sydney autumn is the right time to establish ashwagandha and Rhodiola. Both have growing guides for temperate Australian climates. The dosage and safety sections are also worth reading given the interaction potential with perimenopausal hormonal context.

Adaptogens as Anti-Neuroinflammatory Agents: Mechanism Review Distinguishes HPA-Axis vs Neuroprotective Action
A PMC mini-review examines the neuroinflammatory pathways through which adaptogens affect mood, memory, and anxiety — via HPA axis modulation and cytokine regulation. The review distinguishes adaptogens that work primarily via cortisol downregulation (ashwagandha, Rhodiola) from those with direct neuroprotective action on microglia (Bacopa, Panax ginseng). This mechanistic framing has direct implications for timing: cortisol-pathway adaptogens are optimally taken in the morning; microglial ones show different time-of-day sensitivity.
Why it matters: The neuroinflammatory mechanism also directly overlaps with perimenopausal mood and brain-fog symptoms, which are increasingly linked to oestrogen-driven neuroinflammation. Some adaptogens may be working on both tracks simultaneously for perimenopausal women — which the review acknowledges.
