🔥 The Big One
Women can't get their estrogen patches. The FDA is now scrambling.
Estrogen patch prescriptions more than doubled between 2018 and 2026 — from roughly 5 million to over 11 million — and supply simply hasn't kept up. Since the start of the year, women across the US are hitting empty shelves for the 0.05mg patch, the most commonly prescribed dose. Doctors are improvising: splitting higher-dose patches, stacking two lower-dose ones, or switching patients to estrogen gel at $300/month instead of the usual $35. The FDA confirmed to USA Today this week they are in active talks with the five major patch manufacturers, pressing all to run at full capacity. They won't yet call it a formal shortage — but the language is careful in a way that signals they know exactly how serious it is.
"Women shouldn't have to skip treatment or wonder if they can get their medicine. They're not sleeping, they're having hot flashes again and they're miserable." — Dr. Alexis Dunne, menopause specialist, Chicago
via USA Today, April 17, 2026
Read full story →
Why it matters: HRT prescriptions doubling in under a decade signals a seismic shift in how medicine is treating perimenopause — but the supply chain wasn't ready. This shortage is now a clinical problem; women who finally got on patches are destabilising because they can't refill.
🎯 Why this matters to you You're in early perimenopause, actively researching what intervention — if any — makes sense for you. If you eventually decide HRT is right, the patch is likely what you'd start with. The shortage means your GP may not be able to prescribe what you ask for, and you should know that the alternatives exist (gel, spray, split-dose patches) and are clinically equivalent — just less convenient and sometimes significantly more expensive.

🌿 Women's Health
LONGEVITY MEDICINE
HRT isn't just symptom relief — a new paper argues it's geroprotective at the cellular level
A review published in Aging and Disease proposes a fundamental reframe: the decline in ovarian endocrine function is not a narrow reproductive event but a systems-wide shift in aging biology. The paper argues HRT should be evaluated not as palliation for vasomotor symptoms but as geroprotective intervention — slowing cardiovascular, cognitive, and musculoskeletal decline that accelerates after oestrogen withdrawal. The language is bold by clinical standards; the supporting data on cardiovascular risk, bone density, and cognitive protection is cited carefully. This is the argument that shifts what "appropriate candidate for HRT" means.
Why it matters: The old framing (HRT = symptomatic relief, use minimum dose for minimum time) was already being challenged. This paper gives the opposing view a formal mechanistic argument. If the evidence base holds, the question becomes whether perimenopause is the optimal intervention window — and current data suggests it is.
🎯 Why this matters to you You're working full-time as a hospital scientist, raising two small boys, entering perimenopause. The geroprotective framing matters because it changes the cost-benefit calculation — you're not just weighing symptom management against risk, you're potentially weighing decades of cardiovascular, bone, and cognitive health against the same risk profile. The "window of opportunity" data (starting HRT within 10 years of menopause or before 60) is the part worth reading closely.

LANCET META-ANALYSIS
Lancet: HRT does not increase dementia risk — and doesn't decrease it either
A systematic review and meta-analysis in Lancet Healthy Longevity reviewed all available evidence on menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) and cognitive outcomes. The conclusion: no evidence that MHT either increases or decreases the risk of dementia or mild cognitive impairment. This is significant in both directions — it dismantles the residual anxiety about cognitive risk from WHI-era studies, but it also means the protective narrative (oestrogen is neuroprotective, therefore HRT prevents dementia) isn't well-supported in the post-menopausal period. The reviewers note this reinforces current guidance: prescribe for symptoms and quality of life, not for dementia prevention.
Why it matters: The WHI study's legacy left a generation of GPs and patients overcautious about HRT. This meta-analysis is part of a systematic revision of that evidence base — not to push HRT on everyone, but to give clinicians and patients accurate information to make the decision that's right for them.

A 13-year-old Berlin schoolboy found a 2,300-year-old Greek coin from ancient Troy — in a field in Spandau
While walking arable land in Berlin's Spandau district this week, 13-year-old Luca (a member of a local archaeology hobby group) surfaced a small bronze coin about 12mm across and 7 grams. 🌱 Herbalist's Corner
SYSTEMATIC REVIEW
25 studies, 9 adaptogens: what the evidence actually says about stress adaptation
A systematic review and meta-analysis in ScienceDirect assessed 25 studies across nine adaptogenic herbs: Bacopa monnieri, Eleutherococcus senticosus (Siberian ginseng), Eurycoma longifolia (Tongkat Ali), Gynostemma pentaphyllum, Lepidium peruvianum (Maca), Ocimum sanctum (Holy basil/Tulsi), Panax ginseng, Rhodiola rosea, and Withania somnifera (Ashwagandha). The evidence base varies considerably — Ashwagandha and Rhodiola have the strongest human trial data for HPA axis modulation and cortisol reduction. Bacopa monnieri has good data on cognitive function. The others have more limited evidence or primarily in-vitro/animal data. Critical conclusion: the "adaptogen" category is not homogeneous — mechanism, evidence quality, and safety profiles differ significantly between plants.
Why it matters: If you're planning an apothecary garden and want to grow plants with genuine evidence behind them, the priority list from this review is: Ashwagandha and Rhodiola rosea (strongest stress/cortisol data), Bacopa monnieri (cognitive), Tulsi/Holy basil (adaptogenic + anti-inflammatory). The others are worth growing for other reasons, but the stress-adaptation evidence is thinner.
🎯 Why this matters to you You're actively planning your apothecary garden in Sydney heading into autumn. Ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) is actually well-suited to Sydney's climate — it's a warm-climate plant that can survive mild winters. Holy basil/Tulsi is easy and can be grown as an annual. Bacopa monnieri loves moisture and partial shade. If you're growing for evidence-based function rather than just aesthetics, these three are your highest-priority starts.

Adaptogens as anti-neuroinflammatory agents: what the PMC mini-review actually found
A PMC review specifically on anti-neuroinflammatory effects of adaptogens — relevant because neuroinflammation is implicated in brain fog, mood instability, and cognitive changes during perimenopause. The review found evidence for Ashwagandha and Rhodiola reducing inflammatory markers in animal models; some human data on cognitive improvements likely mediated by inflammation reduction. ⚡ Editor's Corner
The estrogen shortage is a supply chain failure hiding a landmark clinical shift
HRT prescriptions doubling in under a decade isn't a trend — it's a correction. Decades of overcautious post-WHI guidance suppressed a therapy that was appropriate for far more women than it was being given to, and now demand has run ahead of the manufacturers who weren't watching. The shortage will resolve; the shift in clinical thinking that caused it won't reverse. If you're thinking about whether HRT makes sense for you, you're doing it in a window where the evidence base is being substantially rebuilt in real time.
