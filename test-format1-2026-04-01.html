Archaeologists Discover 1,600-Year-Old Roman Concrete — And It's Still Getting Stronger

A team from MIT and Harvard published findings on Roman concrete from a harbour wall in Caesarea that has been submerged for 1,600 years. Not only has it not degraded — it's measurably stronger than when it was poured. The reason: a rare volcanic ash called pozzolana reacts with seawater to grow calcium-aluminium-silicate crystals over centuries. Modern concrete crumbles in 50 years. Theirs was engineered — apparently accidentally — to improve with time.

The science, simply: Roman builders mixed volcanic ash, lime, and seawater. They didn't know why it worked — they just knew it lasted. Modern science now confirms seawater was the secret ingredient, not a flaw. The same material that forms at hydrothermal ocean vents was quietly forming inside their harbour walls.
The dinner-table version: The Romans accidentally built concrete that gets stronger the wetter it gets — for 1,600 years. We've been using concrete that crumbles in 50 years and only just figured out their recipe. Sometimes the old way was better and we just didn't know why.
